# Retrieval practice and desirable difficulties

## Retrieval practice

Trying to recall information from memory strengthens that memory more than reading it again. Answering a question, writing down everything you remember, or explaining an idea without notes are all forms of retrieval. This is called retrieval practice, or the testing effect. In a typical experiment, students read a short text and then either reread it or wrote down what they could recall, without feedback. On a test one week later the recall group remembered more, although the rereading group had felt more confident.

Retrieval helps even without feedback, but feedback makes it work better: after a failed attempt, seeing the correct answer lets the learner fix the error. Flashcards work because each card is a small retrieval attempt followed by feedback.

## The illusion of fluency

Rereading makes a text feel familiar. That familiarity is easy to mistake for knowing: the learner recognises the material but cannot produce it unaided. This is the illusion of fluency. A simple check is to close the book and try to explain the idea or answer a question about it. What cannot be recalled is not yet learned.

## Interleaving

When practising several kinds of problems, mixing them (A B C A C B) instead of doing all problems of one kind before the next (A A A B B B C C C) improves later performance, especially on tests where the problem type is not announced. Interleaving forces the learner to choose the right method for each problem, which is the skill an exam requires. Blocked practice feels smoother and gives better scores during practice, which is why learners tend to prefer it.

## Desirable difficulties

Spacing, retrieval and interleaving share a pattern. They make practice feel harder and slower, and they make performance during practice worse, yet they improve long-term retention and transfer to new problems. The psychologist Robert Bjork called such conditions desirable difficulties. A difficulty is desirable only if the learner can still succeed with effort; a task that is too hard leads to failure without learning.

Measured during practice, the easier methods look better. Measured after a delay, the harder ones win. Judging a study method by how it feels in the moment leads to the wrong choice.
